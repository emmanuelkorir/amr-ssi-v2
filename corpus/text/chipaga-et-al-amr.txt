Prevalence of multi-drug resistant bacteria causing surgical site infection at 
tertiary hospitals in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania: a call for strengthening infection

Damas Chipaga, Mtebe Majigo
, Biswaro Malima and Agricola Joachim
Department of Microbiology and Immunology, Muhimbili University of Health and Allied Sciences, Dar es Salaam, Tanzania
ABSTRACT
Background: Surgical site infections (SSI) are common healthcare-associated infections, yet 
data on causative bacteria and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns are limited. The study 
aimed to determine the bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns among post- 
operative patients at tertiary hospitals in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.
Methods: We conducted a cross-sectional study between September 2022 and February 2023 
to investigate SSI through an aerobic culture of pus aspirates or wound swabs. Isolates were 
identified using conventional methods and an analytical profile index 20E test for 
Enterobacterales. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using Kirby Bauer disc 
diffusion methods. Continuous variables were summarized as mean and standard deviation, 
while categorical variables were summarized as proportions.
Results: A hundred-fifty-eight patients were enrolled, with a mean age of 42.20 ± 12.13 years. 
Of 158,140(88.6%) showed bacterial growth and 164 isolates. Escherichia coli 45 (27.4%), 
Pseudomonas aeruginosa 38 (23.2%), and Staphylococcus aureus 38 (23.2%) were the most 
isolated. Among the Enterobacterales, 39/80 (48.8%) were extended-spectrum beta-lactamase 
producers. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus was detected in 11/38 (29.0%) 
Staphylococcus aureus. Overall, multi-drug resistant strains were 127/164 (77.4%).
Conclusions: Gram-negative bacteria were the predominant cause of SSI. More than three- 
quarters of isolates were multi-drug-resistant strains. The findings underscore the need to 
regularly review antimicrobial prophylaxis practices and treatment guidelines, strengthen 
infection prevention and control measures, and antimicrobial stewardship.

1. Introduction
Surgical site infections (SSI) are common healthcare- 
associated infections [1,2]. Their magnitude differs 
from place to place due to differences in the quality 
of healthcare services [3,4]. The incidence of SSI in 
Low-Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) ranges 
between 10.31% and 16.3% [2]. SSI represents 
a financial burden, especially in LMICs [5], associated 
with extended hospital stays [1]. Patients who develop 
SSI may end up with complications or die [6].
SSI is the most preventable healthcare-associated 
infection when appropriate measures of infection pre­
vention and control (IPC) are applied [7]. Several IPC 
guidelines and tools are available; however, their utiliza­
tion is far from ideal [8]. Hand washing and aseptic 
techniques are the IPC practices in most health facilities. 
Preoperative antimicrobial prophylaxis is commonly 
used to prevent SSI [9]. A single dose of antimicrobials 
administered 1 to 2 hours before incision is adequate for 
most surgical procedures [10]. In our setting, at tertiary 
facilities, the approach to preoperative antibiotic 
prophylaxis involves using a combination of ceftriaxone 
and metronidazole. The benefit of antimicrobial prophy­
laxis may be more significant in settings without com­
prehensive IPC measures [11]. However, inappropriate 
use of antimicrobials influences the emergence of multi­
drug-resistant (MDR) pathogens [12].
SSI may originate from the skin microbiota or the 
gastrointestinal tract flora that gains access through 
a surgical incision [13]. In addition, SSI causative 
agents may originate from contaminated inanimate 
objects at the healthcare service units [13]. Patients’ 
colonization with pathogens from the hospital envir­
onment increases the risk of infection with MDR 
strains [14,15]. Consequently, limited MDR screening 
practices among admitted patients increase the risk of 
MDR colonization and transmission [16]. The emer­
gence of MDR pathogens causing SSI presents chal­
lenges in prevention and treatment and leads to 
increased length of stay and care costs [17].

2. Material and Methods
2.1. Study design and setting
We conducted a descriptive cross-sectional study at 
Muhimbili 
National 
Hospital 
and 
Muhimbili 
Orthopedic Institute in Dar es Salaam between 
September 2022 and February 2023. Muhimbili 
National Hospital has a 1500-bed capacity and attends 
2000 outpatients daily. Muhimbili Orthopedic Institute 
has a 150-bed capacity and attends about 160 patients 
daily.
2.2. Patient recruitment
We enrolled patients admitted and those attending 
postoperative clinics with clinical evidence of SSI 
based on the modified Centers for Disease Control 
and Prevention definition [18]. Recruitment was done 
after obtaining informed consent. The minimum sam­
ple size was estimated using the Kish and Leslie for­
mula, considering 63% SSI with MDR bacteria [19]. The 
minimum required sample size was 143 participants.
2.3. Data and specimen collection
We used a structured data collection tool to extract infor­
mation from patient case notes. Depending on the 
wound’s condition, we collected two pus aspirates or 
swabs from 158 patients. The pus swabs were collected 
aseptically from the depth of the wound using sterile 
cotton wool swabs after cleaning with normal saline. 
The specimens Amies transport media were transported 
to the laboratory for processing within two hours of 
collection.
2.4. Bacterial isolation and identification
We performed Gram staining on one specimen to 
evaluate its quality. The other specimen was inocu­
lated on blood agar and MacConkey agar plates 
(Oxoid, UK) and incubated aerobically at 37°C for 
18–24 hours. Catalase, coagulase, and DNase tests 
were performed for gram-positive cocci isolated on 
blood agar. We identified Gram-negative bacteria 
using conventional biochemical tests (oxidase, tri­
ple sugar iron, indole, urease, and citrate). In addi­
tion, the Analytical Profile Index 20E Test was used 
to identify Enterobacteriales and non-fastidious 
Gram-negative rods.
2.5. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing
We tested antimicrobial susceptibility using the Kirby- 
Bauer disc diffusion method [20]. Briefly, the tests and 
control organisms were suspended in normal saline to 
McFarland 0.5 standard and inoculated on Mueller- 
Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK). Appropriate discs were 
placed, and the plates were incubated at 37°C for 
16–18 hours. We included the following discs for 
Gram-positive bacteria: penicillin (10units), cefoxitin 
(30 μg), gentamicin (10 μg), erythromycin (15 μg), tet­
racycline (30 μg), ciprofloxacin (5 μg), clindamycin 
(2 μg), 
trimethoprim/sulphamethoxazole 
(1.25/ 
23.75 μg), and chloramphenicol (30 μg). The Gram- 
negative bacteria were tested against ampicillin 
(10 μg), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (20/10 μg), cef­
triaxone (30 μg), ceftazidime (30 μg), cefotaxime 
(30 μg), gentamicin (10 μg), tetracycline (30 μg), cipro­
floxacin (5 μg), cefepime (30 μg), piperacillin/tazobac­
tam (100/10 μg), amikacin (30 μg), trimethoprim/ 
sulphamethoxazole (1.25/23.75 μg), chloramphenicol 
(30 μg), and meropenem (10 μg). In addition, the 
broth microdilution method was used to assess the 
susceptibility of Staphylococcus aureus to vancomy­
cin [21].
2.6. Detection of antimicrobial resistance 
phenotypes
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) 
was determined by disc diffusion test using 
a cefoxitin disc (30 μg) on Mueller-Hinton agar, incu­
bated at 35°C for 16–18 hours. A zone of inhibition 
≤21 mm 
was 
considered 
positive. 
We 
used 
Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213 as MRSA positive 
control and ATCC25923 as MRSA negative control 
[20]. Inducible clindamycin resistance (ICR) among 
Staphylococcus aureus resistant to erythromycin was 
detected using the D-test. Erythromycin (15 μg) and 
clindamycin (2 μg) discs were spaced 15 mm apart and 
incubated at 37°C for 16–18 hours. Flattening the zone 
of inhibition adjacent to erythromycin (15 μg) was 
interpreted as ICR. We used Staphylococcus aureus 
ATCC BAA-976 as test negative and Staphylococcus 
aureus ATCC BAA-977 as test positive control [20].
Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) was 
screened by disc diffusion with ceftazidime (30 μg) or 
cefotaxime (30 μg) and confirmed by the double disk 
synergy test. Ceftazidime (30 μg) and cefotaxime 
(30 μg) discs were placed 20 mm from the amoxicil­
lin/clavulanic acid (20/10 μg) disc. The plates were 
incubated at 37°C for 16–18 hours. An enhanced 
zone of inhibition toward the amoxicillin/clavulanic 
acid (20/10 μg) indicated positive ESBL production

2.7. Data analysis
We analyzed data using STATA v.15.1. We described 
continuous variables as mean (± standard deviation)

resistance rates were calculated as a percentage of

defined as non-susceptible to at least one agent in 
three or more antimicrobials categories [23].
3. Results
3.1. Participants’ characteristics
We consecutively enrolled 158 patients with a mean 
age of 42.20 ± 12.13 years. Most, 89 (56.3%) were aged 
25–44, and 82 (51.9%) were from general surgery. 
Most, 113 (71.5%) were males, 86 (54.4%) residents 
of Dar es Salaam, and 85 (53.8%) had a normal body 
mass index. Smokers and alcohol drinkers were 29 
(18.4%) and 24 (15.2%), respectively. Of all, 54 
(34.2%) had comorbidities, 83 (52.5%) had hospital 
admission within six months, and 83 (52.5%) used 
antimicrobial within one month. Ceftriaxone alone 
was given to 77 (48.7%). Most, 97 (61.4%), had limb 
surgery, and only 21 (13.3%) shaved before surgery. 
Clean and clean-contaminated wounds were reported 
in 89 (56.3%) (Table 1).
3.2. Profile of bacterial isolates
Out of 158 samples, 140 (88.6%) had growth of 164 
pathogens; 119 (85.0%) single isolate and 21 (15.0%) 
more than one isolates. Most isolates, 126 (76.8%) 
were Gram-negative. The predominant pathogens 
were Escherichia coli, 45 (27.4%), Pseudomonas aeru­
ginosa, 38 (23.2%), Staphylococcus aureus, 38 (23.2%), 
Klebsiella pneumoniae 25 (15.2%), Acinetobacter bau­
manii, 8 (4.8%), Proteus mirabilis 5 (3.1%) and 
Enterobacter aerogenes 5 (3.1%) (Table 2).

50% to sulphamethoxazole/trimethoprim (82.2–­
100%), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (55.6–100%), and

4. Discussion
The current study revealed that Gram-negative bac­
teria caused more than three-quarters (76.8%) of SSI. 
The resistance of most isolated pathogens to ceftriax­
one was more than 40%. Nearly 8 of 10 Gram-negative 
bacteria and more than three-quarters of bacteria 
causing SSI were MDR. The findings indicate an 
alarming rate of MDR in patients with SSI that require 
immediate action.

The predominance of Gram-negative bacteria in 
the current study was consistent with the previous 
findings from the same setting in 2014 [19]. Similar 
observations were also reported in Ghana in 2020 [24] 
and Uganda in 2019 [16]. A possible reason could be 
contamination with intestinal tract flora during 
abdominal surgery, as Escherichia coli was the predo­
minant bacteria. However, there are variations in dif­
ferent studies. The predominant bacteria in a study in 
Iraq in 2021 was Klebsiella pneumonia [25], while 
a study in India reported Staphylococcus aureus [26]. 
In addition, another study in Tanzania reported 
Pseudomonas aeruginosa [19]. These variations are 
anticipated due to variations in the geographic distri­
bution of causative agents, population, surgical proce­
dure, location of surgery, and the IPC practice.
Participants received antimicrobial prophylaxis, 
either ceftriaxone alone or ceftriaxone and metronida­
zole. A similar practice was reported in Ethiopia 
[27,28]. Our study revealed that over half of Gram- 
negative bacteria were resistant to ceftriaxone. 
Ceftriaxone is also ineffective in treating infection 
caused 
by 
Pseudomonas 
aeruginosa 
and 
Staphylococcus aureus [29,30]. Our finding was com­
parable to a study in Rwanda in 2019, which reported 
53.3% resistance to ceftriaxone [31]. The level of resis­
tance to ceftriaxone observed in the study indicates 
that the current preoperative prophylaxis regimen is 
ineffective. There is a need for appropriate antimicro­
bial prophylaxis as it has been found to prevent 
SSI [32].
The current study found a high resistance rate to 
ampicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, cotrimoxazole, 
and ciprofloxacin. The study in Iraq reported a similar 
antimicrobial susceptibility pattern [25]. The high 
resistance rate to commonly used antimicrobials may 
be caused by widespread and indiscriminate use, poor 
patient compliance, and self-treatment without pre­
scription, as reported in Mwanza 2011 [33]. On the 
other hand, most Gram-negative bacteria were sensi­
tive to amikacin and meropenem, which is consistent 
with the previous studies in India [34,35]. The low 
resistance rate to amikacin and meropenem could be 
explained by the fact that they are rarely used and are 
more expensive.
The proportion of ESBL-producing bacteria was 
comparable to a study in Ethiopia in 2020 [36]. 
Acinetobacter baumannii was highly resistant to most 
antimicrobials. The findings were similar to the study 
conducted in the same hospital in 2014 [19]. The 
similarity implies the persistent spread of MDR 
Acinetobacter baumannii in our setting. The presence 
of MDR bacteria acquired from hospital contamina­
tion indicates poor IPC practices [37]. In short, IPC 
interventions such as hand washing and environmen­
tal decontamination play a significant role in infection 
and MDR reduction [38]. Therefore, there is a need to 
strengthen IPC and antimicrobial stewardship. The 
findings are worrisome as MDR pathogens are diffi­
cult to treat. A similar situation may occur in facilities 
with limited IPC and antimicrobial stewardship prac­
tices, which depend on empirical therapy. Simple 
interventions like hand hygiene, environmental sur­
face cleaning, decontamination, wearing personal pro­
tective equipment, 
and 
continuing education 
effectively prevent SSI [39].
Our study’s limitation was that we did not include 
anaerobic bacterial culture because of limited facilities 
for isolation and identification. In addition, we should 
have followed up with patients to determine their 
outcomes because most isolates were MDR strains. 
Our work was limited to conventional laboratory 
methods, but molecular methods could have been 
useful in detecting virulence and antimicrobial resis­
tance genes.
5. Conclusion
Gram-negative pathogens were the predominant cause 
of SSI, with a relatively high proportion of MDR. The 
pathogens were significantly resistant to ceftriaxone 
used for prophylaxis. The overuse of ceftriaxone as 
preoperative prophylaxis could be the factor behind 
the high prevalence of MDR. We advocate periodic 
reviews of antimicrobial prophylaxis and treatment 
practices, strengthening IPC measures, and antimicro­
bial stewardship. Further studies are recommended to 
evaluate the effectiveness of IPC interventions.

Ethical clearance DA.282/298/01.C/1240 was obtained 
from the Muhimbili University of Health and Allied 
Sciences Research and Publications Committee. We got 
permission from the hospital management. Patients 
gave written informed consent before enrollment. We 
used codes instead of participants’ names to ensure the 
confidentiality of the study participants. The laboratory 
results were reported to the physician for patient care.
