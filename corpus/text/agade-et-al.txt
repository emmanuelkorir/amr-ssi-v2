ABSTRACT
Introduction: Globally, surgical site infection (SSI) remains a major nosocomial infection, contributing significantly to 
morbidity and mortality. Laparotomies are known to have higher reported rates of SSI compared to other types of surgery. SSI 
rates are generally higher in Low‐ and Middle‐Income Countries (LMIC) than in High Income Countries (HIC) for similar 
surgical procedures, though specific rates in LMICs are often unknown. Identifying population‐specific risk factors is critical for 
implementing effective SSI surveillance programs aimed at reducing the infection burden.
Methodology: Data was collected retrospectively from 393 general surgery patients who underwent surgery between January 
2022 and December 2023. The study included all patients aged 14 years and above who had an exploratory laparotomy per­
formed by the general surgery team. Bivariate analysis was performed to establish associations between variables and SSI 
occurrence, followed by a stepwise multivariable logistic regression. SSI was defined as per the World Health Organization 
(WHO) and Center for Disease Control (CDC) criteria.
Findings: The overall complication rate among the 393 patients was 26.7%. The study identified 55 cases of SSI, yielding an 
overall SSI rate of 14.1%. 40.4% of SSI cases were classified as superficial, 32.7% as organ space, and 25% as deep. Wound culture 
was only performed in 24 patients, with Escherichia coli being the most common organism found. Multivariable logistic 
regression identified three independent predictors of increased SSI risk: Operative Time: Each additional hour of operative time 
was associated with a 68% increase in the odds of SSI (aOR = 1.68, 95% CI 1.149–2.455, p = 0.007). Sex: Female patients had 2.6 
times higher odds of developing SSI compared to male patients (aOR = 2.599, 95% CI 1.251–5.399, p = 0.01). Wound Class: Dirty 
wounds were associated with 2.34 times higher odds of infection compared to clean wounds (aOR = 2.343, 95% CI 1.123–4.886, 
p = 0.023).
Conclusion: The SSI rate of 14.1% at MTRH highlights the considerable burden of infection following laparotomies in this 
setting. Operative duration, patient sex, and wound contamination level are critical, modifiable and unmodifiable, independent 
risk factors that should be targeted for surveillance and preventative programs in western Kenya.

Background Information
Surgical site infection (SSI) is globally recognized as the most 
frequent nosocomial infection among patients in hospitals 
worldwide [1]. SSI is a major concern for the patient, the sur­
geon, and the healthcare system, leading to longer hospital 
stays, increased rates of readmission, and a heightened risk of 
mortality [2, 3]. In LMIC, management of SSI adds direct and 
indirect healthcare costs to already strained low resource sur­
gical services in LMIC. It is associated with one‐third of all 
postoperative complications [4] and accounts for 8% of fatalities

caused by hospital‐acquired infections [1]. The World Health 
Organization (WHO) and the Center for Disease Control (CDC) 
define SSI as a superficial or deep infection occurring at the 
surgical site within 30 days, or within 1 year if an implant was 
utilized [5].
Although all surgeries carry a risk of postoperative SSI, typically 
ranging from 2% to 11%, laparotomies have reported rates that 
are notably higher when compared to other types of surgical 
procedures. Furthermore, higher SSI rate has been consistently 
reported in LMIC compared to HIC for comparable surgeries [3, 
6, 7]. A systematic review covering Africa indicated that SSI 
rates generally ranged from 6.8% to 26%, with a predominance 
in general surgical cases [8]. General surgical cases account for 
the majority of surgical site infections [9]. The increase in SSI 
leads directly to an increased length of hospital stay, which 
subsequently translates into increased healthcare costs, thereby 
straining surgical care systems in LMIC such as Kenya [2].
In the existing literature, many risk factors have been identified, 
including unmodifiable patient factors like sex and age, and 
modifiable factors such as tobacco smoking and poor glycemic 
control [10, 11]. Specific operative factors such as longer oper­
ative time (more than 50 min) and wound classification have 
been identified in Tanzania as factors that significantly increase 
the risk of SSI [10]. SSI surveillance programs, which are not 
frequently conducted in LMIC’S6, rely heavily on appropriate 
risk stratification. However, the surgical population western 
Kenya suffers from a lack of specific data regarding SSI 
following laparotomies, which severely restricts the local ca­
pacity to establish effective SSI surveillance protocols.
The objectives of this retrospective observational study were to 
determine the rate of SSI at Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital 
(MTRH) following exploratory laparotomies and to assess po­
tential SSI risk factors within this surgical patient population as 
part of SSI surveillance strategies.
2 |
Methodology
This study utilized a retrospective observational design to 
determine the incidence of SSI following both elective and non‐ 
elective exploratory laparotomies. The research was conducted 
at Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital (MTRH), which is a level 
six healthcare facility situated in western Kenya. Retrospective 
data was collected by reviewing patient records from between 
January 2022 and December 2023. The study population con­
sisted of all patients who underwent exploratory laparotomy by 
the general surgery team at MTRH and were above the age of 14 
years. Data was collected for the duration the patient was 
admitted in the surgical ward. The target sample size was 
calculated using the formula by Fisher et al. (1991). This 
calculation yielded a necessary sample size of 328 study par­
ticipants. The final study cohort included 393 patients. 
Consecutive sampling was employed, where patients were 
identified by reviewing the operation logbooks maintained by 
the general surgery team. Inclusion criteria mandated that pa­
tients be above 14 years old and have undergone an exploratory 
laparotomy by the general surgery team at MTRH. Exclusion 
criteria specifically omitted patients who were referred to 
MTRH for continuing surgical care, including repeat laparot­
omies, if their initial exploratory laparotomies were performed 
at an outside healthcare facility.
The following definitions were applied during the study for 
clarity and consistency:
2.1 |
Surgical Site Infection (SSI)
An infection diagnosed by the surgical team in the postoperative 
continuation notes within 30 days of initial surgery, occurring 
within 30 days on the site where surgery has been done, or 
within 1 year if an implant was used, involving the superficial, 
deep, or organ spaces. A description of the wound, such as 
purulent discharge or fascial/wound dehiscence, was used to 
classify superficial or deep SSI. Radiological reports (CT scan or 
ultrasound) were used to define organ space SSI.
Wound Classification (Based on CDC):
• Clean: Operative wounds that did not involve the respira­
tory, alimentary, genital, or urinary tracts.
• Clean‐contaminated: Operative wounds involving the res­
piratory, alimentary, genital, or urinary tract under 
controlled conditions without significant contamination.
• Contaminated: Open wounds or wounds experiencing a 
breakdown of sterile technique, such as uncontrolled 
spillage from the gastrointestinal tract, or evidence of 
necrotic tissue without purulent drainage.
• Dirty: Old traumatic wounds, infected tissue, or perforated 
viscera.
Urgency of Procedure:
• Elective surgical procedures: Procedures scheduled in 
advance for patients without immediate life‐threatening 
conditions.
• Semi‐elective surgical procedures: Interventions required in 
a timely manner for a surgical condition, but where the 
patient is not acutely life‐threatening.
• Urgent surgical procedures: Procedures for a patient with a 
life‐threatening condition, but the surgery is performed 
once the patient is medically stable.
• Emergent surgical procedures: Procedures that must be 
performed immediately to prevent death or permanent 
disability.
Wound Closure Techniques:
• Primary Wound Closure: Closure of the skin during the 
original operation.
• Delayed Primary Wound Closure: The operative incision is 
left open during the initial surgery but closed primarily 
48–72 h later.
• Secondary Wound Closure: The operative incision is left 
open to close spontaneously by granulation.

2.3 |
Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS with the assis­
tance of a biostatistician. Examination of data included calcu­
lating summary statistics and distributional assessments for 
continuous data, and frequencies and percentages for categori­
cal data. The Chi‐Square Test of Independence or the Fischer's 
Exact test were utilized for bivariate analysis to establish po­
tential associations between the collected variables and the 
incidence of SSI. Subsequently, a stepwise multivariable logistic 
regression analysis was performed to identify independent 
predictors of SSI, with statistical significance set at an alpha 
(type‐2 Error Rate) of 0.05.
2.4 |
Ethical Considerations
Approval was sought and granted by the MTRH institutional 
research and Ethics board; Approval number 004529.
Due to the retrospective nature of the chart review, which 
involved no interventions, procedures, or direct interactions 
with the subject's, informed consent was not obtained. The data 
was de‐identified prior to analysis to mitigate the potential risk 
of breaching patient privacy or confidentiality.
The benefit of conducting this study was to improve the un­
derstanding of complications occurring at MTRH, which would 
facilitate the creation of targeted risk reduction programs.

Results
The study results are based on an analysis of 393 general surgery 
patients who underwent laparotomies at MTRH between 
January 2022 and December 2023. The mean age of the patients 
was 39.5 years (SD 17.3), with ages ranging broadly from 14 to 
97 years. The cohort was composed of 227 males (57.8%) and 
166 females (42.2%). Approximately 28% of the patients had 
documented comorbidities. Malignancies represented the most 
common comorbidity with 48 cases (12.2%), followed by pa­
tients with a history of prior abdominal surgery (25 cases, 6.4%). 
Diabetes Mellitus was present in 10 patients (2.5%), and auto­
immune disease was noted in 2 patients (0.5%). A significant 
portion of the cohort, 121 patients (32.1%), were referrals from 
other health facilities.

accounting for 68.4% (n = 268) of surgeries. Elective surgeries 
constituted 22.7% (n = 89), urgent 5.4% (n = 21), and semi‐ 
elective 3.6% (n = 14).

3.2 |
Postoperative Information and SSI Rate
The overall complication rate for the study cohort was deter­
mined to be 26.7% (95% CI 22.5–31.3). A total of 55 cases of 
surgical site infection (SSI) were reported, resulting in an overall 
SSI rate of 14.1%. SSI cases were classified as superficial in 21 
patients (40.4% of SSIs), organ space in 17 patients (32.7%), and 
deep in 13 patients (25.0%), as demonstrated in Table 2 below.
Wound culture was performed for only 24 patients, representing 
43.6% of those with SSI. Among the results, Escherichia coli was 
the most frequently isolated organism, found in 7 cases (35.0%). 
Other organisms isolated included Candida albicans, Proteus 
vulgaris, Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium, Klebsiella 
pneumoniae spp pneumoniae, Providencia stuartii, and Pseudo­
monas (1 case each, 5.0% each). Notably, five cultures (25.0%) 
showed no growth.
Besides SSI, other complications included fascial dehiscence 
(7.1%, n = 28), mortality (8.7%, n = 34) and respiratory (2.0%, 
n = 8). Anastomotic leak occurred in 5 patients (1.3%), and 
urinary tract infection (UTI) occurred in 1 patient (0.3%).
The average length of hospital stay was 5 days (median IQR 3, 
10), which was the same as the median duration of post­
operative antibiotics prescribed (5 days, IQR 4, 8). Most patients 
(91.0%) were discharged home.
As shown in Tables 3 and 4, bivariate and Multivariate analysis 
examined the relationship between SSI rates and wound clas­
sification, distinguishing between elective and emergent 
procedures.
3.3 |
Elective Laparotomies
The analysis found no statistically significant difference in SSI 
rates across various wound classes following elective laparot­
omies (p = 0.33). Contaminated wounds had the highest 
observed incidence at 20.0%, followed by clean‐contaminated 
wounds at 9.7%. Clean and dirty wounds in the elective cate­
gory reported 0.0% SSI incidence. The interpretation notes that 
the statistical power for this specific cohort was lacking due to 
the extremely small sample sizes, particularly in the clean and 
dirty wound categories.

relationship was identified between wound class and SSI rates 
following emergent laparotomies (p = 0.004). The risk of SSI 
showed a clear, graded increase corresponding to increased 
wound contamination. The SSI rate for clean wounds was 5.7%, 
rising to 8.8% for clean‐contaminated wounds, and 13.6% for 
contaminated wounds. The risk culminated in the dirty wound 
classification, which had the highest SSI rate at 27.1%.

Discussion
The overall surgical site infection rate observed at MTRH 
following exploratory laparotomies was 14.1%. This finding 
aligns with the systematic review conducted across Africa, 
which reported SSI rates ranging from 6.8% to 26% in general 
surgical cases, and supports the initial hypothesis that the SSI 
burden in this LMIC setting would be substantial [8]. A lower 
rate of surgical site infection following laparotomy was reported 
in HIC [7]. The high percentage of emergent procedures (68.4%) 
in this cohort likely contributes to this significant infection 
burden. A significant proportion of the participants presented as 
referrals with perforated viscera or infected tissue, classifying 
them into the higher‐risk wound categories. This was also a 
retrospective review of patients up to the point of discharge. As 
part of the methodology, post discharge SSI were not included in 
the study sample. There are low postoperative visits in LMICS 
[12, 13]. This was the case in most of the post laparotomy pa­
tients in our patient population.
Most of the participants, 98%, had open surgery as opposed to 
minimally invasive surgical approach. Several publications have 
shown a decline in the number of surgical site infections with 
the increase in number of laparoscopic approaches in general 
surgery cases [14–17]. Less than 1% of cases recorded by surgical 
trainees in East, Central and Southern Africa were done lapa­
roscopically with fewer laparoscopy cases in public hospital 
training centers, like MTRH, compared to mission and private 
hospitals [17]. Minimally invasive approaches offer several ad­
vantages that could be beneficial to healthcare systems in 
LMICs [14]. Despite longer operative time in LMIC, laparo­
scopic surgery still offers decreased SSI risk, less blood loss, 
decrease in total cost and earlier hospital discharge compared to 
open surgery [18, 19]. However, access, availability and afford­
ability of laparoscopic surgery services remain low in LMIC, 
including our center [14].

elective laparotomies. In consonance, Prakash et al. reported an

only reported a similar SSI rate of 14% which is this study's 
overall SSI rate [20]. The specific analysis of SSI rate based on 
wound class showed a crucial divergence between elective and 
emergent cases. While elective procedures lacked statistical 
power due to small sample sizes in higher contamination clas­
ses, emergent laparotomies demonstrated a statistically signifi­
cant graded increase in SSI risk corresponding to contamination 
level (p = 0.004). The SSI rate peaked at 27.1% in dirty wounds 
following emergent laparotomies. This emphasizes that emer­
gent surgery involving gross contamination (dirty wounds are 
defined as involving infected tissue or perforated viscera) pre­
sents the highest infection risk in this population.
The multivariable regression identified three crucial indepen­
dent risk factors that remained significant even after controlling 
for confounders: operative time, female sex, and dirty wound 
class. The finding that each additional hour of operative time 
increases the odds of SSI by 68% strongly suggests that efforts 
should be focused on minimizing surgical time to reduce 
exposure and contamination. Several studies report a similar 
association of increased operative time and increased SSI risk 
[10, 11].
The elevated risk observed in female patients (2.6 times higher 
odds) compared to males (aOR = 2.599) is an unmodifiable 
patient factor that warrants further investigation to understand 
underlying population‐specific vulnerabilities. Huda et al. on 
their study on elective laparotomies with more female partici­
pants, did not have any statistically significant association be­
tween sex and SSI risk on bivariate analysis [20]. However, in a 
previous study at this study site, women were more likely to 
have delays in accessing healthcare compared to male patients 
due to financial and cultural factors [21]. Otoki et al. reported 
more postoperative complications, longer hospital length of stay 
in female participants that culminated to higher costs in the 
management of emergency gastrointestinal surgeries in Kenya 
[22]. Patterson et al. in a comparative study also reported worse 
postoperative outcomes in women in LMIC compared to HICs 
probably attributable to health care system failures such as 
surgical workforce [23]. The designation of dirty wounds as an 
independent risk factor (2.34 times higher odds) reinforces the 
need for rigorous intraoperative contamination control and 
optimal antibiotic management tailored to these high‐risk 
cases.
Administration of prophylactic antibiotics is a well‐documented 
practice in SSI prevention [24, 25]. In this study, 95.4% of pa­
tients received prophylactic antibiotics. Only 43.6% of the par­
ticipants who had SSI had microbiological culture and drug 
sensitivity done. This is reflective of the low utilization of cul­
ture and sensitivity in antimicrobial stewardship in LMIC [7, 
24]. The finding that Escherichia coli as the most common or­
ganism isolated from the limited number of wound cultures 
(35.0%) is several other studies [26] suggests targeted prophy­
lactic and therapeutic antibiotic strategies focusing on sensi­
tivity patterns. There is a need to utilize available Microscopy 
Culture and Sensitivity to guide targeted antibiotic use in pro­
phylactic antibiotics and SSI treatment. This would enhance 
stewardship targeted at ensuring and efficacy in SSI prevention 
and in the broader picture of reducing multidrug resistance in 
LMIC [24].
The results from this study may be generalizable to other LMIC 
surgical populations in some aspects. Challenges such as limited 
use of minimally invasive surgical techniques and delayed ac­
cess to surgical care especially in referred patients with 
advanced disease are characteristic of many LMICs. However, 
this was a single center study, limiting its generalizability 
despite the facility being a major referral center in Western 
Kenya receiving patients from a large catchment area. Addi­
tionally, microbiological patterns differ geographically, which 
further limits this study's generalizability.
Limitations of this study include its retrospective nature and the 
duration of follow‐up, which was until patient discharge from 
hospital or mortality. This was due to poor patient compliance 
to attend scheduled postoperative visits in LMICs [12, 13]. Pa­
tients who had implants such as mesh, could have developed 
surgical site infections up to 1 year after the initial surgical 
procedure. Early mortality in the postoperative period before an 
SSI became clinically apparent or recorded could have contrib­
uted to underestimation of the true SSI incidence. This could 
suggest that the SSI rate following laparotomy at MTRH, is 
higher than 14.1%. A prospective multicenter study with a 
longer duration of follow‐up is recommended.
5 |
Conclusion
The SSI rate of 14.1% after laparotomies at Moi Teaching and 
Referral Hospital confirms a significant surgical burden com­
parable to reported rates in other African settings. Operative 
time, female sex, and the presence of a dirty wound are inde­
pendent predictors of SSI in this patient population. To reduce 
the burden of SSI at MTRH, risk reduction programs should 
prioritize minimizing operative duration and enhancing surgical 
practices and antibiotic protocols, especially for emergent pro­
cedures classified as contaminated or dirty.
