Reviewer 2 
This manuscript addresses an important and timely issue in surgical care in East, Central, 
and Southern Africa, namely the relationship between surgical site infections (SSI) and 
antimicrobial resistance (AMR).  
Response: Thank you. 
 
The topic is highly relevant for the region given the growing burden of antimicrobial 
resistance in low- and middle-income country surgical systems. The review has clear 
conceptual value; however, several methodological issues require clarification. 
First, SSI rates are pooled across procedures without accounting for surgical wound 
contamination class (clean, clean-contaminated, contaminated, and dirty) as defined by 
the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Combining these categories may 
introduce substantial confounding and limits interpretation of pooled estimates. 
Subgroup analyses by surgical specialty, contamination class, or hospital level would 
strengthen the findings. 
Response: Thank you for the submission. However, we pooled the rate of SSI cutting 
across all wound classes and hospital levels. There is enough evidence to support this 
approach to documenting pooled rate of SSI across all wound classes. We have 
documented such evidence in this our manuscript. 
 
Second, the comparator defined as “inappropriate antibiotic use or alternative 
antimicrobial regimens” may be difficult to interpret because definitions of inappropriate 
use vary widely across studies. 
Response: Thank you for this observation. We have defined what is meant by 
inappropriate antibiotic use. We understand the complexity of the matter, if considered 
in general. We have attempted to define what we mean, we evidence from other studies 
that have documented this finding. 
 
Finally, the assertion that prophylaxis administered 1–2 hours pre-incision increases SSI 
risk warrants reconsideration, as current WHO guidance supports administration within 
60 minutes and up to 120 minutes for certain antibiotics. In addition, AMR prevalence

Reviewer 3 
This is an important manuscript on a clinically significant problem, and the topic is highly 
relevant for surgeons, microbiologists, and health systems leaders in the region. 
Response: Thank you 
 
 However, the paper currently asks readers to take away a much stronger and broader 
message than the evidence can support. As written, the abstract and conclusion imply 
that roughly one quarter of operations across East, Central, and Southern Africa result in 
SSI and that first-line antibiotics are broadly failing across the region. That is too sweeping 
for the available data. The underlying studies are highly diverse in specialty, wound class, 
hospital type, surveillance method, follow-up duration, and microbiological practice, 
and the pooled SSI estimate sits on extreme heterogeneity with I² of 98.4% and a 
prediction interval from 1.0% to 63.0%. In that setting, the pooled average is at best a 
coarse descriptive summary, not a stable regional estimate, and it should not be 
presented as though it meaningfully represents “all operations” in the region. 
Response: 
 
The manuscript’s central problem is therefore conceptual as much as statistical. The title 
promises a review of “challenges, innovations, and strategies,” but the evidence base is 
dominated by heterogeneous descriptive studies of SSI burden and pathogen resistance, 
with only limited interventional evidence. The paper would be stronger if the authors 
decided more clearly what kind of review this is. If the main aim is to describe burden and 
resistance patterns, the title, objectives, and conclusions should be narrowed 
accordingly. If the aim is truly to review strategies and innovations, then the intervention 
literature is too incomplete to support that framing in its current form.

I do not see several important Ethiopian intervention papers cited, including the five-
hospital Clean Cut pilot in the British Journal of Surgery, the seven-hospital Clean Cut 
scale-up study in JAMA Surgery, and the later stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial of 
Clean Cut for cesarean delivery in Ethiopia. Importantly, these studies do not all point in 
the same direction: the pilot and scale-up studies were associated with reduced SSI, 
whereas the cesarean-delivery trial did not show a reduction in SSI. The problem, 
therefore, is not simply omission of positive studies, but incomplete synthesis of the 
intervention literature as a whole. The authors do include the 2024 Aboderin post hoc 
microbiology analysis from FALCON, but that is not the same as engaging adequately 
with the original FALCON randomized trial as intervention evidence. With the FALCON 
paper, do the authors separate out the non-African countries? 
Response: Thank you for the comments. The study has included a number of papers from 
COSECSA training Countries, including Ethiopian papers, may be not to the satisfaction 
of the reviewer.  
 
The search strategy also appears insufficient for a review intended to speak for the region. 
The stated information sources exclude AJOL, African Index Medicus, and any explicit 
hand-search of ECAJS. That omission likely matters. Based on the authors’ own eligibility 
criteria, which allow studies reporting SSI incidence and/or AMR prevalence, at least one 
relevant ECAJS paper appears potentially eligible and does not appear in the reference 
list: the 2017 Tanzanian orthopaedic study by Kisibo et al. from Muhimbili Orthopaedic 
Institute, which reported a 25.0% SSI prevalence in 300 postoperative patients. I suspect 
there are many others that report SSIs - even if not in the title or abstract. I would therefore 
encourage the authors either to expand the search to include regional databases and 
journals or to state explicitly that this is a review limited to studies captured in major 
international databases rather than a comprehensive regional synthesis. 
The authors do not include this similar paper (PMID: 38626068) which could help to 
strengthen their evidence base and perhaps find other relevant papers. 
A further concern is that the review is not fully reproducible in its present form. The main 
text reports an SSI meta-analysis based on 16 studies and 9,672 patients with a pooled 
incidence of 23%, whereas the appendix GRADE table reports 20 studies, 10,294 
participants, and a pooled incidence of 30.0%. Those are not trivial discrepancies. They 
suggest that the included-study set, the analytic dataset, and the appendices are not 
synchronized. Several citation and study-accounting inconsistencies add to this 
concern. Before any interpretive claims can be trusted, the authors need to perform a 
complete audit of the included studies, figures, tables, appendix counts, and pooled 
analyses and then present one internally consistent dataset throughout.
